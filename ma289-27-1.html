The Pitch

Where would an adversary cut Taiwan’s oil, and can an AI trained on rail tell us?

Taiwan imports nearly all of its crude oil by sea, and every barrel passes through a handful of straits. I am turning those shipping routes into a network model, and using a reinforcement-learning interdiction agent to ask two things: if China put its full effort into cutting Taiwan’s oil supply, where would it act, and can an AI trained on one kind of network reason about a very different one?

Why it matters

Energy is widely identified as Taiwan’s sharpest vulnerability in a blockade or quarantine. The chokepoints are not hypothetical: the Strait of Hormuz, which carried 61% of Taiwan’s crude in 2025, has been largely closed since February 2026, and Taiwan has had to reroute supply. Most open analysis of a Taiwan blockade comes from wargames; this project brings an optimization model to the same question.

Scope

  • In: crude oil only; the four regions that supply it (Persian Gulf, U.S. Gulf Coast, Oman, West Africa); the straits and canals it must pass; Taiwan’s three refinery ports; one month of supply.
  • Out: LNG and coal, detailed military campaigns, escorts and counter-attacks, and anything classified. A proof of concept built from open sources, not a targeting tool.

What I am looking to find

  1. Where the attacker acts. With its full effort (a budget of 100), does China go after distant chokepoints such as Hormuz and Malacca, or Taiwan’s own approaches?
  2. Quarantine versus blockade. How does the answer change between a lower-cost coast-guard quarantine and a higher-cost naval blockade?
  3. Transfer. Does an agent trained on the Ukrainian rail network still find sound strategies on a maritime network without retraining?

Previous work it builds on

  • Network interdiction: the classic optimization models of Wood (1993) and Cormican, Morton and Wood (1998), surveyed in Smith and Song (2020).
  • LTC Webb’s reinforcement-learning approach: Webb and Caplice (2025), which trains an agent to find attacker strategies (defeat, isolate, canalize, divert) on the Ukrainian rail network.
  • Taiwan blockade analysis: CSIS’s quarantine scenarios and its 2025 Lights Out? blockade wargames, which identify energy shipping as the critical factor.
  • Data: the EIA’s Taiwan brief (2026) for import sources and refinery capacity, and IMF PortWatch for shipping traffic.

Goals

  • An open-source, defensible model of Taiwan’s crude oil supply network (built; shown below).
  • A clear picture of where China’s full effort does the most damage, and how that depends on what each action costs.
  • An answer on whether the agent’s learned strategy transfers from rail to sea.
  • A poster presenting the results at the end of the semester.
Initial Findings · 1 November 2026

China’s best plan is a ring of quarantines around Taiwan, and the RL agent can learn it

China spends its full effort budget of 100; Taiwan then reroutes its crude to deliver as much as it can. Each number below is Taiwan’s expected crude delivered, as a share of demand, scored exactly over every combination of which actions succeed. Lower is better for China.

Base case

Greedy leaves Taiwan 70%. The first trained RL agent leaves 62%. The exact optimum leaves about 32%: ten coast-guard quarantines that close every approach to Taiwan’s ports. A retrained agent (run 3) now finds that ring on its own and matches the optimum.

What was unexpected

  • The best plan is a ring, and every piece of it is worthless alone. One quarantine cuts nothing because Taiwan reroutes around it; only the complete ring works. Greedy, local search and the first RL agent all build plans one step at a time, so none of them found it.
  • It is close to home, not at the famous chokepoints. Hormuz and Malacca have detours. Every barrel, whatever its route, has to pass Taiwan’s approaches, so that is the cut that cannot be bypassed. The model found this from the network alone; it matches the encirclement China has rehearsed in recent exercises.
  • The first agent learned fast and then stopped. It settled on its 62% plan within its first 100,000 of 5 million training steps, and all 50 checkpoints scored 62.0 to 63.6%. Playing the ring through the training environment showed why: the first seven quarantines pay nothing, so the agent never saw the ring pay off.
  • A small change fixed it. In run 3, half of the training episodes on Taiwan’s network started with part of the ring already in place. Scored from an empty start, the agent built the full ring itself by 300,000 steps: every checkpoint since plays the same ten quarantines and leaves Taiwan 31.7%, the optimum.

The answer turns on one number

China’s best strategy depends on how reliably a quarantine stops a tanker. Holding the RL agent’s far-away plan fixed (62%, it uses no quarantines) and scoring the ring exactly:

Re-solving for the optimum at p 0.6 and 0.4 returns exactly the RL agent’s plan. Above about 0.65 China should surround Taiwan; below it, act far away. Anything that makes a quarantine less reliable (escorts, reflagging, presence) pushes China back to the far-away plan.

The ring is optimal in every scenario except the cost changes: with cheaper actions China adds the Luzon Strait and the southern South China Sea; with dearer ones it can afford only part of the west-coast cordon. The ring’s share does not move with demand because the approaches have no capacity limit: a path is either open or closed.

How the results were produced

  • RL agent. The Threat-Adaptive Network Interdiction Model (TANIM; Webb and Caplice, 2025), trained on a high-performance computing cluster. A first run practised on random networks and scored 62 to 95% on Taiwan’s network, beating greedy at 6 of 17 checkpoints. A second run practised mostly on Taiwan’s network with its numbers jittered (80% of episodes) and beat greedy at every checkpoint. A third run added the head start described above and matched the optimum.
  • Optimum. Sample average approximation (Cormican, Morton and Wood, 1998): 200 sampled outcomes of which actions succeed turn the problem into one mixed-integer program, solved exactly. Five independent solves give a lower bound of 30.0% (±1.5); the best plan, scored exactly, gives the upper bound of 31.7%. An independent simulation of the ring gave 31.1%.
  • Greedy and random play. Greedy adds the edge with the biggest cut per unit of effort, then tries swaps; random play adds random edges until the budget is spent.

Limits of these results

  • Costs and success chances are open-source estimates and assumptions; the tipping point shows how much the quarantine value matters.
  • No U.S. or allied response, escalation cost, or storage: Taiwan holds about 140 days of crude, and the model measures one month of flow, not how long a disruption lasts.
  • Each action succeeds independently; in practice quarantine ships share a fleet and their outcomes would be correlated.
  • Max-flow assumes Taiwan reroutes perfectly, so every cut shown is a best case for Taiwan.

New questions

  • Can the agent find rings without the hint? Run 3’s head start used the ring the solver found. The next test is the scenarios it has not trained on (cost changes, Hormuz shut), where the best cut is different.
  • Which edges should Taiwan protect first to break the ring?
  • How long must a disruption last to matter, given Taiwan’s storage?
  • Does the same logic hold for LNG, which Taiwan stores for only about 11 days?
The Current Model

61 nodes, 83 edges, every lane on a real sea route

How It Is Built

Four rules that make the network defensible

  • Sources and sinks from EIA data. Four origin regions weighted by 2025 import shares; three ports weighted by refinery capacity (Kaohsiung 670, Mailiao 540, Shalung 200 thousand barrels a day). Demand is 11.9 VLCC loads a month: 783 thousand barrels a day of throughput, divided by 2 million barrels a load.
  • Straits are edges. Every strait and canal runs from its entrance node to its exit node, so an action against it shuts the transit, and there is a node at every junction where routes meet or separate.
  • Every interdiction point has an alternative. Checked automatically on every build. Hormuz has no sea alternative, and China cannot close it from that far away, only slow ships there. So Hormuz is two lanes: 80% of Gulf oil always gets through, and China can remove a 20% delayed share.
  • Real geometry. Lanes are routed on the scgraph sea-lane network, which is too coarse around Taiwan (14 nodes in the whole theater), so the Taiwan lanes use coastline-derived waypoints. No lane crosses land.
How Interdiction Works

One effort budget, a different action on each kind of lane

The attacker spends a single effort budget. Each edge carries the one action that fits where it is, with an effort cost and a probability p that the action shuts the edge, read as the expected share of the month it is closed. That is the same per-edge cost and probability the environment already uses. China’s budget is fixed at 100, so each cost is the percent of its total effort the action uses. p is anchored on real disruptions; costs are ranked by the legal threshold crossed and the distance from PRC bases.

Assumptions and Limits

What the model holds fixed, stated up front

Kept from LTC Webb’s framework

  • Two players, attacker leading and defender following (Stackelberg).
  • Full information, zero-sum, one round.
  • Stochastic outcomes: each action succeeds with its p, and results are expected values.

Added by this network

  • The 2025 supplier mix is held fixed; no spot-market substitution.
  • One month, one commodity: no stock drawdown, rationing, or switch to coal or LNG.
  • No escorts or defender counter-force; the defender re-routes but does not fight back.
  • One action per edge. Results so far use the quarantine variant; the blockade variant is still to run.
  • China always spends its full budget of 100. Effort costs are a ranking scaled to that budget, and the carrier group’s p is assumed; both are varied for sensitivity.
  • Similar nodes are merged: one node per origin region, Kaohsiung and Dalin as one port, Oman and Fujairah as one terminal.
  • Open, unclassified sources only. A proof of concept, not a targeting model.

Still open

  • Shalung’s exact position, which appears in neither PortWatch nor Natural Earth.
Next

How the questions get answered

  1. Done: greedy and random baselines, the exact optimum for every scenario, and two RL training runs on the base case.
  2. Done: diagnosed why the first agent missed the ring, and a third training run with a head-start curriculum that finds it.
  3. Next training run: practice across every scenario, then score the agent on each one, including cases where the best cut is not the base-case ring.
  4. Defender questions: which edges Taiwan should protect, and the blockade variant.
  5. Future work: a traveling salesman problem (TSP) formulation, possibly with k-nearest neighbors, once the current model is working. Not yet decided.

Random and fixed: whether each action works is random (its chance of success); the network, capacities, costs, China’s budget and Taiwan’s best rerouting are fixed.

Products: the results above for every scenario and method, a map of where each method acts, the quarantine tipping point, and the poster.
